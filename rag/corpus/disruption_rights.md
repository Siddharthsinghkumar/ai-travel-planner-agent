# Disruption Rights — Knowledge Base

## EU 261/2004 Compensation Tiers

1. Regulation EC 261/2004 applies to all flights departing from an EU airport (regardless of airline nationality) and all flights arriving at an EU airport operated by an EU carrier. Passengers are entitled to compensation for flight delays of 3+ hours, cancellations, and denied boarding, unless the disruption was caused by extraordinary circumstances.

2. EU 261 compensation amounts are based on flight distance: EUR 250 for flights up to 1,500 km; EUR 400 for flights between 1,500 km and 3,500 km within the EU, and for all other flights between 1,500 km and 3,500 km; EUR 600 for flights over 3,500 km outside the EU. These amounts are fixed and not negotiable.

3. Compensation is reduced by 50% if the airline offers rebooking that arrives at the final destination within a specified time of the original scheduled arrival: within 2 hours for flights up to 1,500 km; within 3 hours for flights between 1,500 km and 3,500 km; within 4 hours for flights over 3,500 km.

4. Extraordinary circumstances that exempt airlines from paying compensation include: severe weather conditions, political instability, security risks, air traffic control restrictions, strikes by third parties (not airline staff), manufacturing defects in aircraft (but not routine maintenance issues), and hidden manufacturing defects.

5. The right to compensation under EU 261 exists independently of the right to care (meals, accommodation) and the right to rebooking or refund. Passengers can claim all three simultaneously. The airline cannot offer care in lieu of compensation or vice versa.

6. Time limits for filing EU 261 claims vary by country: typically 2-6 years from the date of the disrupted flight. In the UK, the limit is 6 years under the Limitation Act. In Germany, it is 3 years. In France, it is 5 years. Passengers should check the specific time limit for the airline's country of registration.

7. EU 261 applies to connecting flights: if a passenger misses a connection due to a delay on the first leg (on the same booking), the compensation is calculated based on the total distance from the original departure to the final destination. If the flights are on separate bookings, each leg is treated independently.

## US Tarmac Delay Rule

8. The US DOT tarmac delay rule prohibits domestic flights from remaining on the tarmac for more than 3 hours without allowing passengers to deplane. International flights have a 4-hour limit. Airlines must provide adequate food and water no later than 2 hours after the tarmac delay begins (unless the pilot determines security or safety reasons prevent it).

9. During a tarmac delay, airlines must ensure that lavatories remain operable and that medical attention is available if needed. The aircraft must remain at a location where passengers can safely deplane during the entire tarmac delay. Airlines can exceed the 3-hour limit only for safety, security, or air traffic control reasons.

10. Airlines that violate the tarmac delay rule can face civil penalties of up to USD 33,842 per passenger per violation. The DOT has levied fines totaling millions of dollars against airlines for tarmac delay violations. Passengers can file complaints with the DOT's Aviation Consumer Protection Division.

11. The tarmac delay rule applies to all US airports and all airlines operating to or from the US, including foreign carriers. It does not apply to flights that are diverted to an alternate airport due to weather or other circumstances beyond the airline's control, although airlines are still expected to provide care.

## Denied Boarding Compensation (US)

12. Involuntary denied boarding (bumping) compensation in the US is based on the delay to the passenger's arrival at their final destination: 0-1 hour delay — no compensation required; 1-2 hour delay (domestic) or 1-4 hour delay (international) — 200% of one-way fare, maximum USD 775; 2+ hour delay (domestic) or 4+ hour delay (international) — 400% of one-way fare, maximum USD 1,550.

13. Airlines must first ask for volunteers to give up their seats in exchange for compensation before involuntarily bumping any passenger. Volunteer compensation is determined by the airline and is typically in the form of travel vouchers. There is no legal maximum for volunteer compensation.

14. Passengers who are involuntarily bumped are entitled to a written statement explaining their rights and how the airline determines who gets bumped. Airlines must provide the compensation in cash or by check (not just travel vouchers), although passengers may accept vouchers voluntarily.

15. The denied boarding compensation rules apply to all flights operating within the US and all US carrier flights internationally. They do not apply to flights on foreign carriers between foreign cities, even if the passenger originated in the US.

## Rebooking Rights

16. When a flight is cancelled, the airline must offer the passenger one of three options: rebooking on the next available flight on the same airline; rebooking on a partner airline or competing carrier if the airline cannot accommodate the passenger within a reasonable time; or a full refund to the original form of payment.

17. Under EU 261, the right to rebooking (rerouting) requires the airline to transport the passenger to their final destination at the earliest opportunity on comparable transport conditions. If the earliest available flight is the next day, the airline must also provide hotel accommodation and transfers.

18. Under US regulations, there is no specific requirement for the airline to rebook on a competitor. However, the DOT expects airlines to make reasonable efforts to accommodate passengers. Some airlines have interline agreements that allow rebooking on competitors during major disruptions.

19. If a passenger is rebooked on a flight that arrives at a different airport than the original (e.g., Newark instead of JFK), the airline must provide transportation between the airports at no cost to the passenger. This is particularly relevant in cities with multiple airports.

## Hotel and Meal Vouchers

20. EU 261 requires airlines to provide care (meals, refreshments, hotel accommodation, and transfers) for passengers experiencing delays of 2+ hours (depending on flight distance), cancellations, or denied boarding. Meal vouchers should be proportional to the waiting time. Hotel accommodation is required when an overnight stay becomes necessary.

21. US airlines are not legally required to provide hotel or meal vouchers for delays, but most major carriers have voluntary policies: American Airlines provides meal vouchers for delays over 3 hours within their control and hotel vouchers for overnight delays within their control. Delta provides similar care. United provides meal vouchers for delays over 3 hours and hotel vouchers for overnight delays within their control.

22. Weather-related delays: Under US regulations, airlines are not required to provide hotel or meal vouchers for delays caused by weather or other extraordinary circumstances. However, some airlines voluntarily provide care even for weather-related disruptions. Under EU 261, care is required regardless of the cause of the disruption, even if compensation is not.

23. Voucher amounts vary by airline and situation: meal vouchers typically range from USD 12-25 per person. Hotel vouchers cover the cost of a standard hotel room near the airport plus transportation. Some airlines provide a flat-rate cash payment instead of vouchers. Passengers should keep receipts for any expenses incurred during a disruption.

## Trip Insurance Interactions

24. Trip cancellation insurance covers non-refundable trip costs if the trip is cancelled due to covered reasons such as illness, injury, death of a family member, natural disasters, or terrorism. It does not cover cancellations due to change of mind or fear of travel. "Cancel for any reason" (CFAR) coverage is available at a higher premium and typically reimburses 50-75% of trip costs.

25. Trip delay insurance covers additional expenses incurred during a covered delay, such as hotel accommodation, meals, and transportation. Coverage typically kicks in after a delay of 6-12 hours and provides USD 100-300 per day for a maximum of 5-10 days. This is separate from the airline's obligations and can provide additional reimbursement.

26. Trip interruption insurance covers the cost of returning home or catching up to a trip if it is interrupted due to a covered reason. This includes the cost of a last-minute one-way flight, unused portion of the trip, and additional accommodation costs. Coverage limits typically range from USD 5,000-50,000.

27. Travel insurance does not replace the airline's legal obligations. Passengers should first seek compensation and care from the airline under applicable regulations (EU 261, US DOT rules), then file an insurance claim for any uncovered expenses. Insurance policies typically require documentation of the airline's response before paying a claim.

28. Credit card travel insurance: Many premium credit cards (Chase Sapphire Reserve, American Express Platinum, Citi Prestige) include trip delay and cancellation insurance as a cardholder benefit. Coverage is typically triggered when the trip was purchased with the card. Benefits vary by card but often include USD 500-10,000 per trip for delays and cancellations.

29. Pre-existing condition waivers: Most travel insurance policies exclude claims related to pre-existing medical conditions unless a waiver is purchased within 10-21 days of the initial trip deposit. The waiver typically requires that the traveler be medically able to travel at the time of purchasing the insurance and that the trip cost be fully insured.

30. Emergency medical evacuation coverage is included in comprehensive travel insurance policies and covers the cost of emergency transportation to the nearest adequate medical facility or back home. Coverage limits typically range from USD 100,000-1,000,000. This is particularly important for travelers visiting remote destinations or countries with limited medical infrastructure.
