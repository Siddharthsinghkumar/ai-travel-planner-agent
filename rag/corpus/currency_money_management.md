# Currency and Money Management Rules — Knowledge Base

1. Currency exchange: Airport currency exchange kiosks typically offer the worst rates (5-15% markup). Banks and local exchange offices offer better rates. ATMs generally offer the best exchange rates (close to the interbank rate) but may charge withdrawal fees of USD 2-5 plus your bank's foreign transaction fee.

2. Foreign transaction fees: Many US credit cards charge 3% foreign transaction fees on purchases made abroad. Cards with no foreign transaction fees include Chase Sapphire Preferred/Reserve, Capital One Venture, American Express Platinum, and most travel-focused cards. Debit cards from Charles Schwab and Fidelity reimburse ATM fees worldwide.

3. Dynamic Currency Conversion (DCC): When paying with a credit card abroad, merchants may offer to charge you in your home currency instead of the local currency. This is DCC and always offers a worse exchange rate (3-8% markup). Always choose to pay in the local currency.

4. Cash vs. card: Many European countries are moving toward cashless societies (Sweden, Norway, UK). However, some countries and situations still require cash: Japan (many small businesses are cash-only), Germany (some restaurants and shops don't accept cards), markets and street vendors worldwide, and tipping in many countries.

5. Traveler's checks: Once popular, traveler's checks are now largely obsolete. Few businesses accept them, and cashing them can be difficult. They have been replaced by prepaid travel cards, credit cards with no foreign transaction fees, and ATM withdrawals.

6. Prepaid travel cards: Cards like Revolut, Wise, and Travelex Money allow you to load multiple currencies and spend at competitive exchange rates. Revolut offers interbank exchange rates on weekdays (with a weekend markup). Wise (formerly TransferWise) offers transparent fees and real exchange rates. These cards are ideal for budget-conscious travelers.

7. Money belts and hidden pouches: While effective for securing cash and documents, money belts can be inconvenient for daily use. A more practical approach is to split cash between multiple locations: some in your wallet, some in a hidden pouch, and some in your hotel safe. This way, if you are pickpocketed, you don't lose everything.

8. Tipping customs worldwide: US (15-20% at restaurants, USD 2-5 for hotel housekeeping), UK (10-15% at restaurants, service often included), France (service included, round up or leave EUR 1-2), Japan (tipping is not expected and can be considered rude), Australia (10% at restaurants, not expected), Thailand (not expected but appreciated, THB 20-50 at restaurants), India (10% at restaurants, INR 50-100 for hotel staff).

9. Budget tracking while traveling: Apps like Trail Wallet, TravelSpend, and Splitwise help track daily expenses. Set a daily budget and track spending against it. Factor in accommodation, food, transportation, activities, and souvenirs. A common rule of thumb is to budget 40% for accommodation, 25% for food, 15% for transportation, 15% for activities, and 5% for souvenirs.

10. Emergency cash reserve: Always carry a reserve of USD 100-200 in small bills (USD 10 and 20) as emergency cash. USD is widely accepted or easily exchangeable in most countries. Keep this reserve separate from your daily spending money. In case of lost cards or closed ATMs, this reserve can cover emergency expenses.

11. Bank notification: Notify your bank and credit card companies of your travel plans before departure. This prevents your cards from being blocked for suspicious foreign transactions. Most banks allow you to set travel notifications through their mobile app or website. Include all countries you plan to visit and your travel dates.

12. Cryptocurrency for travel: Some travel companies accept Bitcoin and other cryptocurrencies. Bitrefill allows you to purchase travel gift cards with crypto. Cryptocurrency ATMs are available in major cities worldwide. However, cryptocurrency is not widely accepted for everyday travel expenses and price volatility makes budgeting difficult.

13. Haggling and bargaining: In many countries (Middle East, Southeast Asia, North Africa, Latin America), haggling is expected at markets and with street vendors. Start at 30-50% of the asking price and negotiate from there. Be respectful and willing to walk away if the price is not right. In Japan, South Korea, and most Western countries, haggling is not practiced in retail stores.

14. Tax-free shopping: Many countries offer tax refunds for non-residents on purchases over a minimum amount (typically EUR 50-175). Request a tax-free form at the store, get it stamped at customs on departure, and submit it for a refund. The refund is typically 70-85% of the VAT amount (the rest is the refund company's fee). Major refund companies include Global Blue and Planet.

15. Currency restrictions: Some countries have restrictions on the amount of local currency that can be brought in or taken out. Argentina restricts USD purchases at official exchange rates. Cuba has a dual currency system (CUP for locals, MLC for tourists). India restricts the amount of Indian Rupees that can be brought in or taken out. Research currency restrictions before travel.
