# Flight Booking and Pricing Rules — Knowledge Base

1. Airline pricing is dynamic and based on demand, seasonality, competition, and booking window. Prices typically increase as the departure date approaches, with the steepest increases occurring within 21 days of departure. Booking 6-8 weeks in advance for domestic flights and 2-4 months for international flights typically yields the best prices.

2. Fare classes (also called booking classes or fare buckets) determine the flexibility and benefits of a ticket. Common fare classes include: Y (full-fare economy, fully refundable), B/H/K/M (discounted economy, partially refundable), Q/V/W (deep discount economy, non-refundable), and J/C/D (business class variants). Each class has different change/cancellation policies and mileage earning rates.

3. Basic Economy is the lowest fare class offered by most major airlines. It typically excludes seat selection, carry-on bags (on some carriers), changes, and upgrades. Basic Economy tickets are non-refundable and non-changeable. The fare difference between Basic Economy and standard Economy is typically USD 20-100 per flight segment.

4. Hidden city ticketing (booking a flight with a connection where your actual destination is the connection point) violates airline contract of carriage and can result in cancellation of return flights and frequent flyer account closure. Airlines actively monitor and penalize this practice.

5. Award flight availability is limited and varies by airline. Airlines typically release a small number of award seats at the lowest mileage level, with more availability at higher mileage levels. Booking award flights 330-365 days in advance (when schedules are released) provides the best availability. Last-minute award availability is rare but can occur.

6. Mileage runs (booking flights specifically to earn elite status miles) are a strategy used by frequent flyers to reach elite status thresholds. The cost per mile should be compared to the value of the elite benefits being pursued. A typical mileage run targets a cost of 1-2 cents per mile.

7. Open-jaw tickets (flying into one city and out of another) are priced similarly to round-trip tickets and can save ground transportation costs. Multi-city tickets are priced as the sum of individual one-way fares and can sometimes be cheaper than booking separate one-way tickets.

8. Error fares (mistake prices published by airlines or OTAs) are occasionally honored by airlines but are not guaranteed. The DOT does not require airlines to honor pricing errors. If you book an error fare, avoid making non-refundable arrangements (hotels, activities) until the airline confirms the ticket.

9. Codeshare flights are operated by one airline but marketed by another. The operating carrier's rules apply for baggage, check-in, and onboard service. The marketing carrier's frequent flyer program may earn miles differently than the operating carrier's program. Always verify the operating carrier before booking.

10. Interline agreements allow airlines to transfer passengers and baggage between each other on separate tickets. Not all airlines have interline agreements. When booking separate tickets, verify that the airlines have an interline agreement to ensure baggage can be transferred and that missed connections may be accommodated.

11. Fuel surcharges are additional fees added to award tickets by some airlines, particularly European carriers. British Airways is known for high fuel surcharges on award flights (can exceed USD 500 for transatlantic Business Class). Some airlines (United, American, Southwest) do not pass fuel surcharges to award travelers.

12. Partner award bookings allow you to use miles from one airline's program to book flights on a partner airline. Star Alliance, OneWorld, and SkyTeam each have extensive partner networks. Partner award availability is often more limited than the airline's own flights and may require calling the airline's reservation line to book.

13. Positioning flights (booking a separate flight to reach a hub with better award availability or pricing) can significantly reduce the cost of award travel. For example, flying from a smaller city to a major hub and then booking the international award from there. Factor in the cost and risk of the positioning flight when calculating total savings.

14. Dynamic pricing for award flights means the number of miles required varies based on demand. United, Delta, and American have all moved to dynamic award pricing. Fixed award charts (where the mileage cost is fixed regardless of demand) are becoming rare but still exist with some programs (e.g., ANA Mileage Club for Star Alliance partners).

15. Stopover rules vary by airline and ticket type. A stopover is a layover of more than 24 hours (international) or 4 hours (domestic). Some airlines allow free stopovers on award tickets (e.g., TAP Air Portugal, Icelandair). Revenue tickets typically do not include free stopovers unless explicitly purchased as a multi-city itinerary.
