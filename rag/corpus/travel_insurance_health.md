# Travel Insurance and Health Rules — Knowledge Base

1. Comprehensive travel insurance typically covers trip cancellation, trip interruption, emergency medical, emergency evacuation, baggage loss, and travel delay. A comprehensive policy for a 2-week international trip typically costs 4-10% of the total trip cost.

2. Emergency medical coverage is the most important component of travel insurance for international travel. US health insurance plans (including Medicare) typically provide little or no coverage outside the United States. Emergency medical coverage limits should be at least USD 100,000 for international travel.

3. Pre-existing condition coverage requires purchasing insurance within 10-21 days of the initial trip deposit and meeting stability requirements (typically no changes to medication or treatment in the 60-180 days before purchase). Without a waiver, pre-existing conditions are excluded.

4. Cancel for Any Reason (CFAR) coverage is an optional upgrade that reimburses 50-75% of non-refundable trip costs if you cancel for a reason not covered by standard policy. CFAR must be purchased within 10-21 days of initial trip deposit and typically increases the policy cost by 40-60%.

5. Rental car insurance through travel insurance provides primary or secondary coverage for collision damage and theft. Primary coverage means you file with the travel insurer first; secondary means you file with your personal auto insurance first. Credit card rental car coverage is typically secondary.

6. Adventure sports coverage is typically excluded from standard travel insurance policies. Activities such as scuba diving (below certain depths), skiing, rock climbing, and bungee jumping require an adventure sports rider. The rider typically costs an additional 10-20% of the base policy premium.

7. Travel insurance claims must be filed within 20-90 days of the incident, depending on the policy. Documentation requirements include receipts, medical records, police reports (for theft), and proof of the reason for cancellation or interruption. Keep all travel documents and receipts.

8. Medical evacuation coverage is separate from emergency medical coverage and covers the cost of transportation to the nearest adequate medical facility or back to your home country. Coverage limits should be at least USD 250,000 for remote destinations. Helicopter evacuations can cost USD 50,000-200,000.

9. Some countries require proof of travel insurance as a condition of entry. Cuba requires all visitors to have medical insurance. Schengen visa applicants must show travel insurance with minimum EUR 30,000 medical coverage. Saudi Arabia requires insurance for tourist visa holders.

10. Travel insurance does not cover losses due to known events (e.g., a hurricane that has already been named before you purchase the policy), travel to countries under a government travel warning, or losses due to intoxication or illegal activities. Read the policy exclusions carefully before purchasing.

11. Vaccination requirements vary by destination. The CDC recommends routine vaccines (MMR, Tdap, influenza) for all travelers. Additional vaccines may be recommended for specific destinations: Hepatitis A and Typhoid for most developing countries, Yellow Fever for parts of Africa and South America, Japanese Encephalitis for rural Asia, and Rabies for travelers who may have animal exposure.

12. Malaria prophylaxis is recommended for travel to many tropical and subtropical regions. Common medications include Atovaquone-Proguanil (Malarone), Doxycycline, and Mefloquine. Each has different side effect profiles and dosing schedules. Consult a travel medicine specialist 4-6 weeks before departure.

13. Travelers' diarrhea is the most common travel-related illness, affecting 30-70% of travelers to developing countries. Prevention includes drinking only bottled or treated water, avoiding raw foods, and washing hands frequently. Treatment includes oral rehydration solutions and antibiotics (typically Azithromycin or Rifaximin) for moderate to severe cases.

14. Altitude sickness can affect travelers ascending to elevations above 2,500 meters (8,200 feet). Symptoms include headache, nausea, fatigue, and dizziness. Prevention includes gradual ascent, staying hydrated, and avoiding alcohol. Acetazolamide (Diamox) can be used for prevention. Severe cases (HAPE or HACE) require immediate descent and medical attention.

15. Deep vein thrombosis (DVT) risk increases during long flights (over 4 hours). Prevention includes staying hydrated, walking the aisle every 2-3 hours, doing seated leg exercises, and wearing compression socks. Travelers with a history of DVT should consult their doctor before long-haul travel.
